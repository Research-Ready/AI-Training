What is Generative AI?

A simple metaphor:

It’s autocomplete on steroids.

It predicts the next most likely word based on patterns in the massive amount of text it was trained on. It doesn’t understand things, but it’s incredibly good at mimicking structure, style, and language.

Let’s demystify the term ‘Generative AI’. Forget complex definitions. The easiest way to think about it is as a super-advanced prediction engine. Just like your phone suggests the next word, AI suggests the next sentence, or the next paragraph. This is key to understanding both its power and its limitations. It’s a pattern-matcher, not a thinking machine.

⚠️ Key Limitation: Hallucinations

AI models can confidently invent facts, citations, or data. They ‘hallucinate’.

Example: An AI might recommend eating one small rock per day for vitamin intake, citing a non-existent medical journal.

Rule #1 of AI: ALWAYS VERIFY.

Before we even write our first prompt, we need to cover the single most important limitation: hallucinations. Because the AI is just predicting the next word, it can very convincingly make things up. It might sound authoritative, but it could be complete nonsense. This is why you, the researcher, are still the most important part of the process. Your critical eye is non-negotiable.

Module 2: Prompting

Learning Objectives

  • Apply the CARE method (Context, Ask, Rules, Examples) to structure your prompts.
  • Use AI for four core research tasks: Summarization, Exploration, Structuring, and Drafting.

Now that you’ve had a first taste, let’s get into the most important skill for working with AI: prompting. This module is all about learning how to ask questions in a way that gets you useful, reliable results. We’ll learn a simple framework and then apply it to some common research tasks.

From Bad… to Better

Imagine your AI assistant is LogiBot, and it’s gone rogue, giving terrible advice.

The Bad Prompt

"Fix the supply chain."

LogiBot’s Bad Answer: “Just ship everything by drone tomorrow. If that fails, tell customers to switch to tea instead.”

(Unrealistic, ignores the actual problem)

The Better Prompt

"Given a port strike in Europe, what alternative transport routes could maintain supply to Asia? List three options with pros and cons for each."

A Better Answer: 1. Air Freight: Fast, but expensive… 2. Rail via land bridge: Cheaper than air, but slower… 3. Sea route around Africa: Avoids the strike, but adds weeks to shipping time…

The quality of your output depends almost entirely on the quality of your input. A vague prompt gets a useless, vague, or even ridiculous answer. A specific, contextualized prompt gets a specific, useful answer. Look at the difference here. The better prompt provides context (port strike), specifies the goal (maintain supply to Asia), and sets rules for the output (three options, pros and cons).

The CARE Prompting Method

The best prompts give the AI everything it needs to succeed. Just remember to CARE.

  • Context: The background information. What does the AI need to know?
  • Ask: The specific task. What do you want it to do?
  • Rules: The constraints. What format, length, or style should it follow?
  • Examples: The desired output. Show it what a good answer looks like.

Here is a simple framework to help you build effective prompts every time. CARE. Before you hit enter, ask yourself: Have I given the AI enough Context? Is my Ask clear? Have I set Rules for the output? Could I provide an Example of what I want? You don’t always need all four, but thinking through them will dramatically improve your results.

DEMO: Applying CARE

Let’s build a prompt together.

Goal: Get a summary of a research paper’s abstract.

  • Context: “You are a research assistant. The following text is a research abstract.”
  • Ask: “Summarize this abstract.”
  • Rules: “The summary must be under 150 words and include the main objective, method, and key finding.”
  • Example: (Optional for this task, as it’s straightforward)

Final Prompt: You are a research assistant. Summarize the following abstract in under 150 words. The summary must include the main objective, the method, and the key finding. [Paste abstract here]

I’ll demonstrate how this works in practice. I’m going to take an abstract, and instead of just saying “summarize this,” I’m going to build a CARE prompt. I’ll define the persona, state the context, give it a clear task, and set rules for the output. Watch how the result is much more structured and useful than if I had used a simple, one-line prompt.

TASK 1: Summarization

Goal: Create a clear, concise summary of a research abstract.

  1. Find a research abstract in your field.
  2. Upload it or paste it into OpenWebUI.
  3. Use a CARE prompt to ask for a summary.
    • Context: “You are a research assistant…”
    • Ask: “Summarize this…”
    • Rules: “…in 150 words, capturing the main question, method, and findings.”
  4. Review the result. How did it do?

Your turn. Find an abstract from a paper you know. Use the prompt structure we just discussed to ask for a summary. The key here is to practice applying the ‘Rules’ part of the CARE framework. Don’t just ask for a summary; ask for a summary of a specific length that contains specific elements. Then, critically evaluate the output.

TASK 2: Exploration

Goal: Ask deeper questions about a research document.

  1. Upload a full paper or report you are familiar with.
  2. Ask targeted questions using CARE prompts:
    • “What is the main research question and why does it matter, according to the introduction?”
    • “List the datasets and methods used, as described in the methodology section.”
    • “Extract the three key findings from the results section, with supporting quotes.”
  3. Verify the AI’s answers against the source document.

Now let’s go beyond summarization. The real power of AI in research is for exploration. Take a paper and ask it specific, targeted questions. Notice how these prompts are much more powerful than “what is this paper about?”. We’re directing the AI to specific sections and asking it to perform specific tasks, like listing or extracting. Remember Rule #1: Always verify the answers.

TASK 3: Structuring Notes

Goal: Turn unstructured text into a clean, reusable table.

  1. Upload a chunk of text from your notes or a paper.
  2. Ask OpenWebUI to structure it.

Prompt: Organize the key concepts from the following text into a Markdown table with three columns: "Concept", "Definition", and "Example from Text". [Paste text here]

  1. Review the table. Is it accurate? Can you copy and paste it into your own work?

This is one of the most practical, time-saving uses of AI. We all have messy notes. This exercise shows you how to get the AI to do the tedious work of organizing them. The key is specifying the exact format you want, in this case, a Markdown table with specific column headers. This makes the output immediately usable.

TASK 4: Drafting

Goal: Use AI to create a first draft of a section, based on evidence.

  1. Upload a paper or your structured notes from the previous task.
  2. Ask the AI to draft a short section.

Prompt: Using ONLY the information provided in the uploaded document, draft a 200-word "Background" section for a research paper. It should introduce the topic, state the main problem, and lead into the research question. Use placeholders like [Author, Year] for citations.

  1. Review the draft. Does it stick to the source material?

Let’s tackle writing. The key here is to use the AI as a starting point, not a final writer. This prompt is carefully constructed to be safe. We’re telling it to ONLY use the information we provided, which reduces the risk of hallucination. We are also asking for citation placeholders, reminding ourselves that we need to add the real references. Never copy-paste AI-generated text into your work without a thorough review and editing process.

Case Study: AI in Supply Chains

AI isn’t just academic. It’s transforming industries.

  • Amazon uses AI to forecast demand for 400M+ products.
  • DHL uses AI to predict shipping delays and reroute freight.
  • Maersk uses AI to reduce vessel downtime and cut CO₂ emissions.

This shows the power of using data and predictive models to make better, faster decisions.

Let’s look at a real-world example from supply chain management. Companies like Amazon and DHL are using AI on a massive scale to do things that are conceptually similar to what we’ve just practiced: they take in data (context), ask questions (ask), and generate predictions or plans (output). This is a great illustration of how these skills are relevant beyond academia.

⚠️ Key Limitation: Bias

AI models are trained on human-generated text, so they inherit our biases.

If historical data shows that managers are predominantly male, an AI might be more likely to use male pronouns when discussing “a manager.”

Rule #2 of AI: ALWAYS question the output for hidden assumptions.

This is another critical limitation to be aware of. AI is not objective. It learns from the vast, messy, and biased world of text on the internet. This means it can perpetuate and even amplify stereotypes. As a researcher, it’s your job to be on the lookout for this and to critically assess whether the AI’s output is fair and unbiased.

DISCUSSION: Ethics & Privacy

  • 🔒 Confidentiality: Should you upload unpublished data to a public AI? (Hint: No.)
  • ⚖️ Authorship: When does using AI cross the line from ‘assistance’ to ‘plagiarism’?
  • 🛡️ Accountability: If an AI makes a mistake in your research, who is responsible?

Let’s take a few minutes to discuss some of these important ethical questions. I’ll break you into small groups. I want you to talk about these three points. There are no easy answers here, but it’s crucial that we, as a research community, engage with these questions thoughtfully. (After discussion) The consensus is clear: use private systems like OpenWebUI for sensitive data, always disclose your use of AI, and remember that you, the researcher, are always the one who is ultimately accountable for your work.

The VCH Research Journey

AI can support you at every step of the 8-step Theory U process.

  1. Problem ID -> AI can summarize stakeholder interviews.
  2. Data Collection -> AI can help structure raw data.
  3. Ideation -> AI can brainstorm potential solutions.
  4. Validation -> AI can draft feedback surveys.
  5. Proposal -> AI can help draft your MVP document.
  6. Engagement -> AI can summarize community feedback.
  7. Testing -> AI can help document test results.
  8. Scaling -> AI can draft implementation plans.

For those of you familiar with the VCH Theory U process, think about how AI can plug into each stage. It’s a tool that can assist you all the way through, from understanding the problem to scaling the solution. It’s particularly powerful in the early stages for sense-making and in the later stages for documentation and reporting.
